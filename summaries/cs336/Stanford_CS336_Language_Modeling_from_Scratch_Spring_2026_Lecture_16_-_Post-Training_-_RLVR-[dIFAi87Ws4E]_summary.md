### 1. Executive Summary & Core Concepts

**Lecture Overview:**
This lecture addresses the transition from RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) to RLVR (Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards) as a solution to the over-optimization bottlenecks in language model post-training. It posits that for verifiable domains like mathematics and coding, we can replace noisy human preference models with robust, rule-based or compiler-based rewards. The lecture details the algorithmic shift from complex PPO implementations to the simpler, more stable GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) and analyzes how this shift enabled open-source models (DeepSeek, Kimi, Qwen) to achieve "thinking" capabilities comparable to closed-source frontier models.

**Key Concepts Highlight:**
*   **RLVR (Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards):** A post-training paradigm where the reward signal is derived from objective, verifiable outcomes (e.g., passing a unit test, solving a math equation) rather than subjective human preferences. This allows for scalable, compute-heavy optimization without the annotation bottlenecks of RLHF.
*   **PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization):** The traditional "workhorse" of RL. While conceptually simple, it is notoriously difficult to implement due to high variance, the need for a separate value network (critic), and sensitivity to implementation details (clipping, baselines).
*   **GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization):** An alternative to PPO introduced by DeepSeek. It eliminates the value network entirely, replacing it with a "group-relative" advantage calculation (z-scoring rewards within a batch of rollouts). This drastically reduces memory usage and implementation complexity.
*   **Outcome vs. Process Supervision:** A critical design choice in RLVR. **Outcome supervision** rewards only the final answer (binary correct/incorrect). **Process supervision** rewards intermediate steps. Recent evidence suggests outcome supervision is often sufficient and more scalable, as process reward models are hard to build and scale.
*   **Length Normalization Bias:** A known artifact in standard GRPO implementations where dividing the reward by sequence length encourages the model to generate longer, "rambling" chain-of-thought (CoT) outputs, particularly when failing to solve a problem.
*   **Curriculum Filtering (Best-of-K):** A data curation strategy where problems are filtered based on the model's current success rate. Problems that are too easy (always solved) or too hard (never solved) are removed to ensure the RL process receives meaningful gradient signals.
*   **The "Thinking" Pipeline:** The architectural composition of modern reasoning models: Base Model $\rightarrow$ Long-CoT SFT (often via distillation) $\rightarrow$ RLVR (GRPO) $\rightarrow$ RLHF (for style/formatting).

### 2. Deep Dive: Expanded Lecture Notes (The "Teaching" Section)

#### Concept 1: The Limitations of PPO in Language Modeling
*   **Detailed Explanation:** PPO is a policy gradient method that uses a "clipped" surrogate objective to ensure stable updates. However, in LLMs, PPO requires a **Value Network** (a critic model) to estimate the value of states (tokens) to compute advantages. This value network is as large as the policy model, doubling memory requirements. Furthermore, PPO is highly sensitive to implementation details; subtle differences in how baselines or clipping are handled can lead to fundamentally different optimization trajectories.
*   **Context & Nuance:** In domains like Go (AlphaGo), PPO works well because the reward (win/loss) is sparse but verifiable, and the state space is structured. In LLMs, the "state" is a sequence of tokens, and the reward is often delayed until the end of the sequence. The lecture highlights that while PPO is the theoretical standard, it is often "too painful" for modern LLM scaling, leading to a desire for alternatives like DPO or GRPO.
*   **Analogy:** Think of PPO as driving a car using a complex dashboard that requires a separate engineer (the value network) to constantly interpret the engine's temperature. If the engineer makes a mistake, the car drives into a ditch. GRPO is like driving with a simple GPS that just says, "You are 5 miles from the destination," which is easier to maintain.
*   **Key Takeaway:** PPO is powerful but brittle; its reliance on a separate value network makes it memory-intensive and prone to implementation errors in LLM contexts.

#### Concept 2: GRPO – The Simplification
*   **Detailed Explanation:** GRPO (from DeepSeek) removes the value network. Instead of comparing a rollout to a predicted value, it compares a rollout to **other rollouts** generated for the same prompt. The advantage $A_i$ is calculated as the z-score of the reward within a group of $G$ samples: $A_i = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(r)}{\text{std}(r)}$. This acts as a dynamic baseline.
*   **Context & Nuance:** Theoretically, this is a deviation from standard policy gradients because it normalizes by standard deviation, which is not a valid baseline subtraction in the strict mathematical sense (it changes the direction of the gradient). However, empirically, it works remarkably well. The "clipping" mechanism in PPO becomes irrelevant in the online GRPO setting because the ratio of the new policy to the old policy is 1.
*   **Analogy:** In PPO, a student gets a grade compared to a predicted grade. In GRPO, a student gets a grade compared to the *class average* for that specific exam. If you score higher than the class, you get a positive advantage; if lower, negative.
*   **Key Takeaway:** GRPO replaces the expensive value network with a statistical baseline (z-scoring within a group), making it a "one-page implementation" that is far easier to deploy.

#### Concept 3: The Impact of Normalization on Chain-of-Thought
*   **Detailed Explanation:** A critical nuance in GRPO is the **length normalization** (dividing reward by sequence length).
    *   *The Problem:* If the model is wrong, it generates a long CoT. Dividing a negative reward by a large length number makes the penalty very small. This incentivizes the model to "ramble" or generate infinitely long incorrect outputs to dilute the penalty.
    *   *The Consequence:* This leads to "length explosion" where models think longer and longer without improving accuracy.
    *   *The Fix:* Newer approaches (like Kimi) explicitly add length penalties or compressive rewards to prevent this, aiming for "shortest correct path."
*   **Context & Nuance:** The lecture notes that while "longer thinking" looks impressive, it is often an artifact of the algorithm rather than true reasoning. The "Aha!" moment observed in DeepSeek R1 was initially hyped, but the professor argues it may simply be a side-effect of the model emitting math tokens due to pre-training, not necessarily a new cognitive capability unlocked by RL.
*   **Analogy:** Imagine a test where you get a penalty for wrong answers, but the penalty is divided by the number of words you write. To minimize the penalty, you would write a 10-page essay of nonsense rather than admit you don't know the answer.
*   **Key Takeaway:** Length normalization in GRPO can inadvertently encourage verbose, inefficient reasoning; modern systems must actively counteract this to ensure computational efficiency.

13. **RL vs. Distillation:** RL is strictly necessary when you do **not** have high-quality long-CoT data to train on (i.e., for frontier problems like Olympiad math). If high-quality reasoning traces exist (e.g., from a larger model), Distillation/SFT is often sufficient and cheaper. RL is the "generator" of supervision; Distillation is the "consumer."
